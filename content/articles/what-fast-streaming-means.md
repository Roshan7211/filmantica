FAST stands for free ad-supported television, and it describes services funded by advertising rather than subscription. The label is industry jargon, but the thing it names has become one of the main ways films reach audiences, and it is worth understanding because it explains a great deal about what you can watch for nothing.

## The two shapes it takes

FAST covers two arrangements that feel different in use.

**On-demand catalogues** work like any streaming service — a library you browse, choose from, and watch with advertising breaks. This is what most people encounter, and it is what makes up the free catalogues on the major services in India.

**Linear channels** are the stranger half: continuously running streams organised by theme, which you join wherever they happen to be. A channel of westerns, or courtroom drama, or a single long-running series playing in order. There is no choosing, only tuning in.

The linear format sounds like a step backwards, and its persistence surprises people. It survives because choosing is work. A viewer who wants something on rather than something specific is well served by a channel and poorly served by a grid of ten thousand titles.

## Why it grew

For most of the last decade, streaming growth meant subscription growth. That has slowed, for a straightforward reason: households reach a limit on how many services they will pay for, and most markets are at or near it.

Services responded by looking for revenue from viewers who would not add another subscription. Advertising is that revenue. This is why several major subscription services have added advertising tiers, and why free catalogues have grown rather than being wound down.

From the rights holders' side, the pressure runs the same way. A studio with a deep library earns nothing from titles nobody is licensing. Ad-supported placement turns a dormant asset into modest but real income, and modest income across thousands of titles is worth having.

Both pressures point the same direction, which is why the free catalogue has been getting larger rather than smaller.

## What the advertising actually buys

The economics are worth understanding because they determine what ends up free.

Advertising on a television-sized screen commands more than advertising on a small one, and streaming advertising carries better targeting and measurement than broadcast. That makes the rates decent — but decent is not the same as a subscription, which generates several times more per viewer.

The consequence is a catalogue shaped by cost. Ad revenue comfortably funds licensing library titles, genre films, and older series. It does not fund expensive originals, current theatrical releases, or prestige drama, and no amount of growth in the model changes that arithmetic.

This is the honest reason free catalogues look the way they do. It is not a lack of ambition; it is that the sums only close on content that was inexpensive to license.

## What it means for viewers

Three practical consequences.

**The free catalogue is getting better.** More rights holders are placing more content on ad-supported services than five years ago, and the trend is continuing. A judgement about free streaming formed a few years ago is probably out of date.

**Advertising is becoming harder to avoid.** As subscription services add advertising tiers, the ad-free experience is increasingly the premium one rather than the default. The gap between paying and not paying is narrowing in terms of what you watch and widening in terms of interruption.

**Fragmentation is not improving.** More services carrying more free content means more places to check, not fewer. The catalogue grows; finding things in it does not get easier.

## The linear channel oddity

It is worth returning to the linear format, because its survival says something about how people actually watch.

A large grid of choices produces decision fatigue. Anyone who has spent twenty minutes browsing and then watched nothing has experienced the failure mode. A channel removes the decision entirely — something is on, and you either want it or you do not.

There is also a real social dimension. Watching something that is simply on, rather than something selected, is a different experience from watching something chosen. Broadcast television had that quality and streaming largely removed it. Linear FAST channels are, oddly, the format bringing it back.

Whether this appeals is temperamental. But it explains why services keep launching channels rather than only expanding catalogues.

## Where India sits

India's free ad-supported market is unusually well developed, for reasons specific to the market.

Price sensitivity is real, which makes advertising-funded models more viable than in markets where subscriptions are easily afforded. The advertising base is large. And the volume of domestically produced content across many languages gives services a deep, inexpensive supply of material that international rights holders are not competing for.

The result is that the free catalogue in India is larger relative to the paid one than in most markets, and considerably stronger in regional-language content than international coverage suggests.

## What to expect next

Two things seem reasonably safe to predict.

Free catalogues will keep growing, because both pressures driving them — slowing subscription growth and dormant libraries — are structural rather than temporary.

And the boundary between free and paid will keep blurring, as more subscription services add advertising tiers and more free services add paid ones. The question a viewer faces is shifting from "free or paid" toward "how much advertising am I willing to accept", which is a different and more granular decision.

## Why the jargon is worth knowing

FAST is an industry term and nobody needs to use it in conversation. But knowing the category exists, and understanding that it is a deliberate business model rather than a leftover, changes how you read a free catalogue.

It is not a bargain bin. It is a distribution window with its own economics, carrying content chosen because those economics work — which is precisely why it is deep in older and genre film, strong in regional content, and empty of this month's releases.

Used with that expectation, it delivers considerably more than its reputation suggests.

## How it differs from broadcast television

The obvious comparison is to television, and the differences are instructive.

Broadcast is constrained by spectrum: a finite number of channels, each expensive, each needing an audience large enough to justify the slot. That economics forces programming toward broad appeal and pushes anything niche off the schedule entirely.

FAST has no such constraint. A channel costs almost nothing to run, so a service can operate hundreds, including ones serving audiences far too small for broadcast. This is why free streaming carries channels devoted to a single long-running series, or to a genre that no broadcaster would give an hour to.

The second difference is measurement. Broadcast advertising was sold against estimates from sample panels. Streaming advertising is sold against actual playback data. That precision raises the rates, which is a meaningful part of why the model works at all now when it would not have a decade ago.

## The thing this model does badly

Discovery, and it is worth being blunt about it.

A catalogue assembled from whatever was inexpensive to license has no editorial shape. There is no commissioning, no season, no through-line — just a large number of titles that happened to be available. Recommendation systems trained on viewing history partly compensate, but they optimise for what resembles what you already watched, which is the opposite of discovery.

The linear channels are, in their way, an attempt to solve this: somebody chose an order, which is a minimal form of curation. It is not much, but a channel of film noir playing in sequence is more of an editorial position than an unsorted grid of ten thousand titles.

This gap is why third-party guides exist and why they are useful. The services are good at holding content and poor at helping anyone find it, and those are genuinely different problems.

## What a viewer should take from all this

The useful shift is to stop thinking of free streaming as the cheap version of paid streaming and start thinking of it as a different window with its own logic.

Paid services are built to acquire and retain subscribers, so they spend on exclusivity and on the titles people will switch providers for. Free services are built to accumulate viewing hours cheaply, so they spend on breadth and on titles nobody is fighting over.

Neither is better. They are answers to different questions. If the question is "I want to watch the thing everyone is discussing", paid wins outright. If the question is "I want something worth two hours tonight", free is frequently the deeper catalogue, and it costs nothing to look.

The mistake is asking the first question of a free service and concluding the model is inadequate. It is not inadequate; it is aimed elsewhere.
