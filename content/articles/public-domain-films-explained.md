Some films belong to everyone. Copyright is not perpetual, and once it expires a work enters the public domain, where anyone may copy, show, sell or adapt it without asking permission.

It is a genuinely useful category, and it is also the subject of more confident misinformation than almost any other aspect of film rights.

## The rule in India

Under Indian law, a cinematograph film is protected for sixty years from publication. The clock starts at the beginning of the year following release.

That means films published in 1965 entered the Indian public domain in January 2026, and each new year brings another.

Sixty years is comparatively short. Many countries run considerably longer, which is where most of the confusion begins.

## The same film, different answers

The United States protects films for ninety-five years from publication. So in 2026 a film from 1960 is public domain in India and still protected in America.

Neither answer is wrong. Copyright is territorial, and a work can be free in one country while protected in another. There is no international public domain.

This matters practically for anyone hosting films online, because a website is reachable everywhere. A film that may lawfully be shared with viewers in India may not lawfully be shared with viewers elsewhere, and the correct response is to restrict by territory rather than to assume the shorter term applies globally.

## The mistakes people make

**Assuming old means free.** Age is necessary but not sufficient. A 1955 film is public domain in India but is unlikely to be in Europe or America.

**Assuming free-to-find means free-to-use.** A film being downloadable says nothing about its copyright status. Availability and permission are entirely separate questions.

**Trusting a label.** Public domain claims attached to uploads on archive sites are typically made by whoever uploaded the file, not verified by anyone. Many are simply wrong.

**Forgetting the parts.** A film contains other works — a score, a screenplay, sometimes source material. These carry their own terms, which can outlast the film's. A film whose copyright has expired may still contain music that is protected, which is a well-documented trap.

**Assuming a restoration is free.** A newly restored print may attract its own protection even when the underlying film is public domain. The old film is free; that particular restoration may not be.

## What is actually in the public domain

In India, essentially anything published before 1966. That includes a great deal of early Indian cinema and a substantial amount of international film from the silent era through the early 1960s.

The practical problem is not legal but physical. Many films from that period survive in poor condition, in incomplete prints, or not at all. What is available online is often a transfer from whatever material survived, and the quality reflects that.

There is a second category worth knowing about: films whose copyright lapsed for procedural reasons rather than age. Under older American law, works could fall into the public domain if formalities were not observed, which is why certain mid-century American films are freely available while their contemporaries are not. This does not apply under Indian law, which has no equivalent formality requirement.

## Why it matters for a streaming site

Public domain is the only category where a site can host and distribute a film itself without licensing anything.

That sounds like an opportunity and is mostly a constraint. Because copyright terms are long, the public domain contains nothing recent, and a catalogue built on it is by definition a catalogue of old films. It cannot be otherwise, however much one might wish it.

This is worth stating plainly because the alternative belief — that there is some legal route to hosting recent films for free — leads people toward sites that are not legal at all.

## Where to actually find them

Public domain films are widely available, particularly through archives that specialise in preservation. Quality varies enormously between a properly restored transfer and a low-resolution copy of a copy.

If you are looking for a specific old film, it is worth checking whether a restored version exists rather than settling for the first copy you find. The difference is frequently the difference between a watchable film and an unwatchable one.

## Why terms are as long as they are

Sixty years in India, ninety-five in the United States, seventy years after the author's death across much of Europe. These are not arbitrary, but neither are they derived from any principle about how long a film remains commercially valuable.

Copyright terms have extended repeatedly over the last century, generally at the point when a small number of highly valuable works were about to expire. The argument made for extension is that longer protection encourages creation. The argument against is that essentially no film is made in the expectation of revenue sixty years hence, so the extension rewards owners rather than incentivising creators.

Whatever one thinks of that, the practical effect is that the public domain grows slowly and its contents are old. It is worth understanding this rather than expecting the boundary to move.

## Public domain is not the same as freely licensed

A related category is often confused with it: works whose creators have chosen to license them permissively while copyright still runs.

Creative Commons licences are the common form. A film released under one is still under copyright — the owner has simply granted permission in advance, on stated conditions. Those conditions vary and matter. Some require attribution. Some prohibit commercial use, which includes an advertising-supported website. Some require derivative works to carry the same licence.

This distinction catches people out constantly. A Creative Commons licence is a grant with terms attached; the public domain has no terms because there is no owner left to impose any.

There is also a marker called the Public Domain Mark, which is worth treating carefully. It is a label indicating that someone believes a work is already out of copyright. It conveys nothing itself and is frequently applied incorrectly.

## Restorations, and why they complicate things

A film from 1930 may be in the public domain while a 2019 restoration of it is not.

Restoration can involve substantial creative work — colour grading decisions, reconstructing missing footage, remastering audio. Depending on jurisdiction and the extent of the work, that effort can attract its own protection covering that particular version.

The underlying film remains free. A different print of it remains free. But the specific restored version may not be, which is why archives sometimes offer an old transfer freely while a restored release is sold commercially.

For a viewer this rarely matters. For anyone hosting or distributing films it matters a great deal, and it is the sort of detail that turns a confident assumption into a problem.

## What this means if you are building something

If you intend to host public domain films rather than simply watch them, three rules cover most of the risk.

Verify by age against the law of the territory you serve, rather than trusting any label attached to a file. Restrict distribution to territories where the term has actually expired, since a website reaches everywhere and the shortest term does not govern. And check the components — score, screenplay, source novel — because a film can be free while something inside it is not.

None of this is exotic. It is simply that "public domain" describes a legal status that has to be established, not a property that can be inferred from a file being available.

## The honest summary

The public domain is real, legally unambiguous, and useful. It is also old, incomplete, and smaller than people assume, with edges — scores, screenplays, restorations — that catch out the careless.

It is a good place to find early cinema. It is not, and will never be, a source of recent films.

## Where to start if you are curious

The most rewarding entry point is early cinema rather than mid-century film, because the material is unambiguously free everywhere and the best of it has been properly restored by archives with an interest in preservation. Silent comedy in particular survives in far better condition than its age suggests.

## One thing to carry away

Age alone never establishes that a film is free to use. The territory, the components, and the specific version all bear on it, and the only reliable method is to check rather than to infer from a file being easy to find.

## A final distinction

Public domain describes the absence of an owner. Free-to-watch describes a commercial arrangement made by an owner who still exists. Both get you a film at no cost, and confusing them is the source of nearly every mistake in this area — including some made confidently and in print.
