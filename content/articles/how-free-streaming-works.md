Somebody paid for the film you are about to watch for nothing. Understanding who, and why, explains most of what is otherwise puzzling about free streaming — why certain films appear and others never do, why titles vanish, and why the free catalogue in India looks nothing like the free catalogue in Britain.

## The model has a name

Services funded by advertising rather than subscription are known in the industry as FAST — free ad-supported television. The category covers everything from Plex and Amazon MX Player to the free tiers of services better known for their paid ones, like Hotstar and Zee5.

The arrangement is straightforward. The service licenses a film from whoever owns it, usually for a fixed period and a fixed territory. It then shows the film to you with advertising breaks, and sells that advertising. If the advertising revenue over the licence period exceeds what the licence cost, the deal worked.

That single calculation explains most of what follows.

## Why some films are free and others never will be

A film's value to a rights holder is highest at release and declines from there. The industry manages this decline deliberately, through what are called release windows: cinema first, then digital purchase and rental, then subscription streaming, then — eventually — free ad-supported streaming.

Each window exists because the film earns more in it than it would in the next one. Nobody puts a film on a free service while people are still paying to rent it, because doing so destroys the rental revenue for a smaller advertising return.

So the films you find free are generally films whose more lucrative windows have closed. That is why free catalogues skew toward titles a few years old, and why a film currently in cinemas is never legitimately free anywhere.

There are exceptions worth knowing about. Independent films without a distributor often go straight to free services, because reach matters more to their makers than a rental window that would earn very little anyway. Documentaries reach free services faster than fiction, since their commercial window is short and their relevance is long. And regional cinema, particularly in India, moves onto free platforms quickly because the advertising market for it is strong and the international rental market is thin.

## Why films disappear

The most common complaint about free streaming is that a film you meant to watch is gone.

This is not a fault. It is the licence expiring.

A service does not own the film. It has bought the right to show it for a period — often a year, sometimes less. When that period ends the film comes down, unless the deal is renewed. Sometimes it moves to a competitor who bid more. Sometimes the rights holder pulls it back to build a catalogue for a service of its own.

The practical consequence for a viewer is that a free catalogue is a snapshot rather than a library. Something you noticed last month may not be there now, and something absent last month may have arrived.

It also explains why availability information goes stale so easily, and why any site that tells you where to watch something has to check its data constantly rather than filing it once.

## Why the catalogue differs by country

Rights are sold territory by territory. A studio might license a film to one service in India, a different service in Britain, and keep it for itself in the United States.

This is why a film free on an Indian service may be a paid rental in America, and why a recommendation from an American publication is often useless locally. It is also why a service that operates in many countries shows a different catalogue in each — the service is not choosing, the licences are.

For viewers in India this cuts favourably. The free ad-supported market here is unusually well developed, with Hotstar, Amazon MX Player, Zee5, Hungama Play and Plex all operating substantial free catalogues, and a large amount of Indian and regional cinema licensed to them.

## What the advertising is actually paying for

It is reasonable to ask whether the advertising load is a fair trade. A feature film on a free service typically carries several breaks, which is broadly comparable to broadcast television and noticeably less than the same film would carry on television twenty years ago.

The revenue per viewer is small — considerably less than a subscription would generate — which is why free services tend to carry deep back catalogues rather than expensive new titles. The economics only work at volume and with content that was cheap to license.

This is also the honest answer to why free services rarely have the specific film you are thinking of. The films most people want are the ones whose rights are most expensive, and those are exactly the ones that do not fit an advertising-funded model.

## How to use free services well

Given all of that, a few habits make free streaming considerably more rewarding.

**Browse rather than search.** Searching for a specific film usually ends in disappointment, because the specific film you thought of is probably in a paid window. Browsing what is actually free tends to surface things you would not have thought to look for.

**Check more than one service.** The catalogues barely overlap, since each service licensed different titles. A film absent from one is frequently present on another.

**Watch things when you notice them.** Licences expire without announcement. A film you add to a mental list for next month may not be there.

**Treat availability information as perishable.** Any list of what is free, including ours, is accurate as of when it was last checked. Good sites check often and tell you when they last did.

## The genuinely useful part

The reason this model is worth understanding is that free streaming is not a lesser version of paid streaming. It is a different thing, funded differently, and it is best at different films.

It will not give you the film everyone is talking about this week. It will give you a large, strange, deep catalogue of films from the last few decades that you would otherwise never have come across, at no cost, entirely legally. Used on its own terms, that is a considerably better deal than it first appears.
